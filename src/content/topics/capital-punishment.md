---
title: Capital punishment
question: Should the death penalty remain available as a punishment?
domain: politics
status: draft
updated: 2026-09-06
positions:
  - id: retention
    name: Retention
    summary: The state should keep capital punishment available for the most severe crimes.
    case:
      - type: values
        text: >-
          Some crimes are severe enough that any lesser punishment fails to answer them.
          Proportional desert is a moral claim, not a policy forecast, and it does not
          depend on deterrence working.
      - type: values
        text: >-
          Victims' families and the wider community hold a legitimate claim to a response
          that is proportionate to the harm done to them.
      - type: empirical
        text: >-
          The availability of a capital charge gives prosecutors leverage that resolves
          cases without trial and secures cooperation from co-defendants.
        source: Plea-bargaining literature; see Kuziemko (2006) and subsequent replications.
        caveat: Genuinely contested. The same literature is used to argue capital charges coerce false pleas.
      - type: empirical
        text: >-
          Life imprisonment is not risk-free. Prisoners already serving life without parole
          have killed guards and other inmates, with no further sentence available to deter them.
        source: Bureau of Justice Statistics, Mortality in Local Jails and State Prisons series.
    cost:
      - type: empirical
        text: >-
          The system has condemned innocent people. More than 200 people have been exonerated
          and released from death row since 1973.
        source: Death Penalty Information Center, Innocence Database.
        caveat: DPIC is an abolitionist organisation. Retentionists dispute individual case classifications, not the phenomenon.
      - type: empirical
        text: >-
          The deterrence argument has largely collapsed. The National Research Council's 2012
          review concluded that existing studies are uninformative about whether capital
          punishment deters homicide, and many retentionists have since retreated to pure desert.
        source: National Research Council, Deterrence and the Death Penalty (2012).
      - type: empirical
        text: >-
          Capital cases cost more than life imprisonment, not less, because of mandatory
          appellate review. The taxpayer argument runs against retention.
        source: State-level cost studies, e.g. Kansas Judicial Council (2014), Washington State (Collins et al., 2015).
      - type: values
        text: >-
          Application varies with the county that charges the case and the race of the victim.
          A punishment that depends on where you offend is difficult to defend as desert.
  - id: abolition
    name: Abolition
    summary: The state should not hold the power to execute, and the death penalty should end.
    case:
      - type: values
        text: >-
          The state should not hold the power to deliberately kill its own citizens. The
          objection is to the existence of the power, not to how carefully it is exercised.
      - type: values
        text: >-
          An irreversible punishment administered by a system known to make errors cannot be
          justified, however rare those errors turn out to be.
      - type: empirical
        text: >-
          Application tracks the race of the victim and the discretion of individual county
          prosecutors, not only the severity of the crime.
        source: Baldus, Woodworth & Pulaski (1990); General Accounting Office review (1990).
      - type: empirical
        text: >-
          Decades of mandatory appeals impose prolonged uncertainty on the victims' families
          the punishment is meant to serve, often outliving them.
        source: Bureau of Justice Statistics, Capital Punishment series - median time from sentence to execution.
    cost:
      - type: values
        text: >-
          Abolition asks victims' families to accept a response they may experience as
          inadequate. That grievance is a real moral cost, not an irrational impulse to be
          educated away.
      - type: values
        text: >-
          Life without parole, abolition's usual substitute, is itself a severe sentence that
          abolitionists scrutinise far less closely than the one they oppose.
      - type: predictive
        text: >-
          The cost and deterrence arguments are contingent. If capital cases became cheap and
          deterrence were demonstrated, those arguments would vanish, which suggests they were
          never the real reason for the position.
      - type: predictive
        text: >-
          Total abolition forecloses any distinct response to the extreme cases - mass murder,
          terrorism - where public support is strongest and least easily explained away.
crux:
  question: Does the fallibility of the institution override what the offender deserves?
  explanation: >-
    Both sides largely agree that the system makes mistakes, and both largely agree that some
    crimes deserve death in principle. The disagreement is about which of those two facts
    governs the other. Almost everything else on this page is downstream of that one question.
  pivot: >-
    A retentionist who accepted that no system can be made accurate enough becomes an
    abolitionist. An abolitionist who accepted that desert is owed regardless of institutional
    risk becomes a retentionist.
revisions:

This page covers the death penalty as a punishment available to the state, in the American
legal context. It does not cover method of execution, the separate question of extrajudicial
or wartime killing, or the death penalty in jurisdictions where it is applied to non-homicide
offences - each of those changes the argument enough to need its own page.

Both positions here are held by serious people with worked-out reasons. If you finish this page
and one side looks obviously stupid, we have failed to write it properly. Tell us so.
